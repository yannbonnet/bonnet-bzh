One recommendation succeeded: computer science became a taught discipline, through the
"sciences numériques et technologie" course and then the "numérique et sciences
informatiques" specialisation introduced toward the end of the decade. The outcome,
however, only imperfectly matches the intention: the report aimed at a general,
critical digital culture; the reform produced a track of excellence for pupils already
headed toward the sciences.

The report had correctly identified the sticking point — teacher training — and that
remained the blind spot. March 2020 provided a brutal demonstration of it: educational
continuity relied on individual improvisation and on tools that had been neither chosen
nor mastered, six years after a report that had called precisely for this competence to
be built. A report that names the right obstacle and fails to get it removed is a
report that failed, whatever the accuracy of its diagnosis.

What has aged worst is the underlying assumptions. The 2014 text shared its era's
optimism about the effects of digital equipment in the classroom. Research conducted
since has not confirmed that link, and several countries have since restricted screen
use in schools. The report was probably right to want computer science taught, and
wrong to believe that equipment alone would transform pedagogy.
